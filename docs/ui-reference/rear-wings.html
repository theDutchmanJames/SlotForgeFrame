How it is put together

  • A wing is four parts, each with its own tab: the wing itself (span, chord, profile, angle, a Gurney flap, an optional second element), the endplates (a shape and its size), the legs (swan neck from above, or posts from below, how many, how far apart, how tall) and how it is fixed to the body.
  • Fixing to the body is the part that matters most on a slotcar. Each leg ends in one or two pins that go through holes in the body. Melted: the pin is longer than the body wall by about its own diameter, and that bit is melted over from inside into a small mushroom head. Glued: short locating pins, or a flat foot that is glued on top of the body. A collar on the pin (optional) sits on top of the body, so the wing always stands at the same height.
  • The body is drawn in the section: its wall thickness (hard plastic or Lexan) and the angle of the deck the legs stand on, so the legs come out the right length and the pins stand square to the body.
  • Ready-made wings to start from, sized for 1/32 or 1/24: GT3 swan neck, GT3 on posts, LMP, DTM with two elements, Group C, and a classic flat wing. They are taken from the proportions of the real cars (a GT3 wing is about 1.8 m wide with a 300 mm chord: 56 × 9.5 mm in 1/32).
  • Checks: a wing, a leg or an endplate thinner than your nozzle can print, a trailing edge too thin, legs too slender for their height, pins too thin to melt or too short for the wall, endplates that do not cover the wing's ends, and a wing wider than your rules allow.
  • Print: in one piece, upside down so the wing's top lies on the bed (swan neck), or as a kit: the wing with its endplates, and the legs apart, which slot in. A drilling template (a strip with the hole spacing) helps put the holes in the body in the right place.

Ideas beyond the brief

  • On the body: Frame already loads the body from SlotForge Anvil. The wing can stand on it: drag it along the tail and the legs follow the body's surface, each one cut to the right length, the pins square to the skin.
  • Mount spots shared with Anvil, as the body mounts already are: Anvil can then put the holes (or small sockets for the pins) in a printed body itself.
  • Downforce has no meaning at this scale, but the angle of the wing still changes its look; the angle and the Gurney flap are there for that, not for grip.
  • Weight: a wing sits high and at the very back. Frame can show its weight, and how much it moves the centre of gravity (it already works that out for the chassis).
